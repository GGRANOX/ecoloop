About the project

Why ECOLOOP?

Small Actions. Big Impact. A student project that shows how classic AI algorithms turn a photo into a responsible decision.

The problem

Wrong sorting ruins good recycling

When a plastic bottle ends up with paper, or greasy food packaging lands in the recycling bin, the whole batch can be contaminated. Contaminated loads are often rejected and sent to landfill or incineration — even the items that were sorted correctly.

People are unsure which bin is right, rules differ between cities, and manual sorting at facilities is slow, expensive and unpleasant work.

Goals

  • Recognise plastic vs paper from a photo
  • Make the AI's reasoning transparent
  • Teach sorting through play

Role of AI

Computer vision sees the material; probability theory measures how sure we are; decision theory chooses the safest action; search plans the movement.

Benefits of automation

  • Fewer contaminated batches
  • Consistent 24/7 sorting
  • Safer jobs for people
  • Data about waste streams
For the presentation

The algorithms, explained simply

0 · CNN — the eyes (MobileNetV2, transfer learning)

A convolutional neural network pre-trained on ImageNet already knows edges, textures and shapes. We replace its last layer with two outputs (plastic, paper) and fine-tune it on the TrashNet photos. The softmax output — for example P(plastic)=0.83, P(paper)=0.17 — is the evidence for the next steps.

Until trained weights exist, a transparent DEMO analyser (colour, highlights, texture) is used and clearly marked.

1 · Bayes' theorem — how sure are we?

P(C|E) = P(E|C) · P(C) / P(E)

Prior P(C) — what we believe before seeing the photo (e.g. 45% of items in the bin are plastic). Likelihood P(E|C) — how likely the classifier's output is if the item really is plastic or paper (taken from the model's confusion matrix). P(E) — the total probability of this evidence, used to normalise.

Analogy: a doctor combines how common a disease is with how reliable a test is. A positive result from an unreliable test should not make us 100% sure.

2 · MDP — what should the robot do?

A Markov Decision Process describes states (ItemDetected, MaterialIdentified, LowConfidence, ReadyToSort), actions (Sort Plastic, Sort Paper, Inspect Again), transition probabilities and rewards (+10 correct, −10 wrong, −1 inspect). Value iteration repeatedly applies the Bellman equation until the values stop changing, giving the optimal policy.

Analogy: if you are not sure which exam answer is right and a wrong answer costs a lot, it is worth spending a minute re-reading the question. The discount factor γ says how patient the robot is.

3 · A* — how does the robot get there?

f(n) = g(n) + h(n)

g(n) is the distance already travelled, h(n) the Manhattan-distance estimate to the bin. A* always expands the most promising cell first (open list), remembers explored cells (closed list) and — because h never overestimates — returns the shortest path, or reports that no path exists.

Analogy: a GPS that prefers roads heading towards your destination instead of trying every street in the city.
